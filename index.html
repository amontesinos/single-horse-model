The fine print: what the geometry actually proves

This illustrates a possibility; it does not prove that real horses share a 4D source. The model is deliberately constructed to cast a horse-shaped projection at zero rotation. Explaining real horses would require evidence and a physical mechanism, neither of which this page supplies.

Let H be our 3D horse, built as a union of convex polyhedra. We construct a four-dimensional solid by extending every point of H along the spatial coordinate w. For a = 0.58:

H₄ = H × [−a, +a]
Rₓw(θ): x′ = x cos θ − w sin θ; w′ = x sin θ + w cos θ
π(x′, y′, z′, w′) = (x′, y′, z′)
Therefore π(H₄) = H when θ = 0.

The left panel uses an oblique 4D-to-3D diagram: (x′ + 0.56w′, y′ + 0.68w′, z′ + 0.42w′), followed by a 3D camera view. The right panel uses orthographic projection along w, followed by that same camera. It displays the full projected solid, not just a central slice. Each convex component is projected by taking the 3D convex hull of both w-boundaries. Overlapping components form the horse.

A tesseract is specifically the 4D analogue of a cube. Our “horse tesseract” is a horse-shaped 4D extrusion, a playful analogy. The fourth dimension here is an extra direction in space, not time. “Shadow” means a geometric projection; no physical 4D light source is being claimed.

In Edwin A. Abbott’s Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions (1884), a two-dimensional observer encounters a higher-dimensional world. The book’s sphere passing through a plane produces cross-sections; this demo instead explores projections. Those are different ways to represent a higher-dimensional object.

A continuous projection of this connected hyperhorse is connected. It cannot produce every separated horse on Earth under one ordinary projection. The “single horse” story therefore needs additional assumptions beyond this model. Your coworker is allowed to bring that up.
